noisestate · three players on a triangle

Who should be seen

A public signal on aggregate action makes every player worse off — unless it exposes only one of them, and then it matters a great deal which one.

reveal the incumbent, not the contender

Exposing the player who already dominates de-escalates and beats no signal at all. Exposing the best-informed player tightens the deadlock and can push best-response dynamics past ρ = 1.

1The game

Three players share one two-dimensional state and each pulls it toward its own vertex of a triangle. They are asymmetric on both margins: precision p = (3, 10, 6) and effort cost r = (0.5, 1.0, 1.5), so the cheapest actor is also the worst informed. Targets enter the loss linearly, so they set the means and leave the kernels — and the best-response Jacobian — alone. The spectrum comes from precision and cost.

Player 1 wins the tug-of-war at every configuration below: the equilibrium mean sits nearest its vertex, never the centroid. Cheap action beats sharp information in deciding where the state rests.

5Where the mean goes

Being loud drags the equilibrium toward your own vertex — but by spending more, not by being obeyed. Projecting the movement onto each vertex direction, relative to the balanced case:

p2 loud: → its own vertex +0.1458, rivals' −0.1099, −0.0359    effort 1.709 → 2.188 and player 1's effort rises too, 1.980 → 2.272. Both push harder; p2 wins the larger share of the escalation.

Player 1 is the exception, and it explains the whole result. Being loud moves the mean almost nowhere (|dx| = 0.0057, and slightly away from its vertex), yet every player's effort falls. Player 1 already dominates, so revealing its action changes no outcome — it only removes uncertainty the others were paying to hedge against. Revealing a contender escalates; revealing the incumbent de-escalates.

6Against the dissertation's information starvation

Chapter 2 finds that concentrating precision on the efficient mover shifts the mean toward that player's target, sharply reduces destructive effort, and lowers both players' costs — an information monopoly beating balanced precision, with effort costs (r1, r2) = (0.05, 0.2). What carries over

Information starvation survives the move from two players to three and from a scalar state to a triangle in the plane. But its sign is a statement about the cheap-effort regime, not a general one: the effort reduction grows from 0.6% to 7.7% as effort gets cheaper, and only at the far end does it outweigh the statistical loss of blinding two players.

7Where a fixed precision budget should go

Section 4 asked who should be seen. The mirror question is who should see: given a fixed total precision to hand out, how should a planner split it? Sweeping 91 allocations of a budget of 15 across the simplex, at both effort scales, gives the same answer twice.

It is an interior tilt toward the cheapest actor, not a monopoly. Player 1 takes 65% of the budget in the stationary regime and 73% in the cheap-effort one, and in both cases handing that player everything is worse than stopping short of it.

Two details sharpen it. First, the tilt steepens as effort gets cheaper — 65% to 73%, with the gain over balance growing from 3.3% to 5.4%. The corner solution of the chapter's two-player result is the limit this is heading toward, not a separate regime.

Second, at the finite optimum player 2 gets less than player 3, 1.44 against 2.62, even though player 3's control is the dearer one. The planner starves the middle player hardest, and player 2 is the incumbent's most dangerous rival: cheaper to act than player 3, and by default the best informed. That is the same player section 4 said not to expose. Both instruments name the same target.

The expensive mistake

The worst allocation in both regimes is all precision to player 3, 62% and 83% above the optimum. It is not over-concentration that hurts — it is concentrating on the wrong player. Starving the efficient mover is what the dissertation warns about, and it is the largest effect on either map.

8What is not settled

The stationary formulation has no equilibrium at cheap effort. Halving the costs breaks it: the tail stops decaying (ratio 1.85 against 0.38), the solve stops converging, and cost grows with the window rather than settling — an undiscounted tug-of-war over an integrator has unbounded average cost. Every cheap-effort result above is therefore finite-horizon.

The σf = 0.25 row has ρ > 1 and has not had the warm-start check that distinguishes a second branch from the equilibrium, so treat it as indicative. And an earlier version of the comparison in section 6 was wrong: effort costs were passed as a Python default argument, so two different regimes returned byte-identical numbers. That impossibility is what exposed it; the table above is after the fix.
